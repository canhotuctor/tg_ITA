\section{Motivation}

An autonomous robot has to turn a stream of noisy, partial measurements into
a picture of its surroundings, anticipate how that picture will change,
and commit to an action before the world has finished moving.  This loop runs
continuously, under uncertainty, and with immediate physical consequences.
The same demand appears whether the robot is sorting parts on a
factory line, inspecting a pipeline on the seabed, or driving across another
planet: perceive a changing world and decide what to do next.

% \color{red}
% figura 1: some robotics examples
% \color{black}

For decades, the dominant answer to that demand was to anticipate the world on
the engineer's behalf.  Behavior was spelled out in advance as rules, state
machines, and tuned heuristics, each branch encoding a designer's expectation
of what the robot would face.  Inside a tightly controlled setting this strategy
is hard to beat.  It degrades quickly, however, as the number of situations
grows: every new contingency must be foreseen and written down by hand, the
resulting logic becomes difficult to maintain, and little of the effort carries
over when the task or the environment changes.

A different answer matured over the past decade.  Instead of prescribing
behavior, \gls{drl} lets an agent discover it, by repeatedly acting in an
environment and adjusting toward whatever earns reward.  The class of
problems it can handle has widened steadily, and these problems can be ordered by
how closely they resemble the physical world a robot inhabits.

At the structured extreme sit fully observable, turn-based games with discrete
moves.  Go is the canonical example: a player sees the entire board, acts only
on their turn, and chooses among a finite set of legal moves, yet the game hides
on the order of $10^{170}$ positions behind those simple rules.  In 2015
AlphaGo became the first program to defeat a professional human
player~\cite{silver2016mastering}, and its successor AlphaGo Zero reached the
same standard learning purely from self-play, without a single human game as a
reference~\cite{silver2017mastering}.

%% FIGURE SUGGESTION 1: AlphaGo: the Lee Sedol match photograph (2016).
%% Widely available under press license from DeepMind; cite the original source.

Relaxing those assumptions one at a time brings the setting closer to robotics.
Let many agents act simultaneously, in continuous time, while hiding part of the
state from each of them, and the problem changes qualitatively.
AlphaStar reached this regime in StarCraft~II (Figure~\ref{fig:starcraft_2_game}),
where an agent must interleave long-horizon economic planning with split-second
control over individual units, select among roughly $10^{26}$ actions at every
step, and do so without ever observing the full board~\cite{starcraft_marl}.  Its robustness
came not from optimizing against a single adversary but from training inside a
\emph{league} of continually evolving rivals, so that no one exploitable
strategy could survive.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Cap1/starcraft2.png}
    \caption{A picture of the StarCraft 2 game. It is possible to see a clash between units of Zerg and Terran factions. Credit: Blizzard Entertainment (\url{https://blizzard.com/}).}
    \label{fig:starcraft_2_game}
\end{figure}

None of these results involves a body.  A
simulated agent issues an action and the action simply happens; a physical one
must produce torques that move mass, manage balance and contact, and recover
when the world reacts in ways no rule foresaw.
Continuous sensorimotor control on real hardware is where
progress is now most visible.
Promotional material from Boston Dynamics shows its Atlas humanoid performing soccer
skills (Figure~\ref{fig:atlas_rabona}), which suggests how far dynamic, whole-body coordination on hardware has come.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Cap1/atlas_rabona.png}

    The robot can be seen doing a "Rabona", also known as "chute de letra" in Portuguese.
    Credit: Hyundai and Boston Dynamics (\url{https://bostondynamics.com/atlas}).}
    \label{fig:atlas_rabona}
\end{figure}

Robot soccer draws together, in one compact and well-defined domain, the
challenges discussed above: continuous control, real-time decision-making,
multi-agent cooperation, and adversarial competition.  It is cheap enough to
simulate at scale, yet the underlying decision problem remains hard, which
makes it a useful benchmark for techniques whose ultimate target is the
physical world.

\section{Contextualization}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

In the context of Robot Soccer, various robotics leagues and competitions have emerged in the last decades.
One of the popular leagues in Brazil is the IEEE \gls{vsss},
a category in which two teams of
differential-drive robots play on a flat field.  A camera mounted above the
field estimates the positions of all robots and the ball; an off-board computer
processes these estimates and sends wheel-speed commands to each robot via
radio.  In the 3v3 format considered here, each team has three homogeneous
robots and no kicking mechanism, so effective team behavior must arise from
ball conduction, positioning, and coordinated movement.
Figure~\ref{fig:vsss_game_diagram} shows the common IEEE VSSS game setup.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{Cap1/vsss_game.png}
    \caption{IEEE VSSS game set. Credit:~\cite{ieee_vsss_rules}.}
    \label{fig:vsss_game_diagram}
\end{figure}

The ITAndroids \gls{vsss} team currently relies on a classical robotics stack
for strategy, path planning, and low-level control: a hierarchy of
hand-crafted rules and heuristics that govern how each robot navigates the
field and responds to game events. Figure~\ref{fig:vss_robots} shows a picture of the ITAndroids robots and the overhead-vision marker. This stack performs well in practice but
has fundamental limitations.  Rule-based strategies are designed for a finite
set of anticipated situations and do not automatically improve with more
data or competition experience.  Adapting them to a new opponent or a changed
team composition requires manual re-engineering.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Cap1/vss.jpeg}
    \caption{ITAndroids \acrshort{vsss} robots and overhead-vision marker.}
    \label{fig:vss_robots}
\end{figure}

The potential of learned strategies for this domain has been demonstrated by
a series of prior works, each addressing a different scope. \textcite{bassani2020}
developed an open RL framework for \gls{vsss} and showed that
a trained single-agent policy could match the level of human-designed
strategies, reaching fourth place at the 2019 Latin American Robotics
Competition (LARC). \textcite{medeiros2020} applied \gls{ppo}
with curriculum learning to teach a single robot to intercept the ball in the
presence of an opponent, outperforming both handcrafted baselines and agents
trained without the curriculum.  \textcite{tg_thay} extended
this framework to penalty kicks, achieving conversion rates substantially above
the team's existing kicker.  Subsequent work by \textcite{master_thay} moved to an adversarial multi-agent setting, studying how
different opponent-sampling strategies affect the quality of the learned policy
in penalty-kick scenarios.  \textcite{brandao2022} went
further, applying \gls{ppo} with self-play to a full 3v3 team, simultaneously
learning control and strategy, and reporting that the resulting policy
defeated the best heuristic of the Pequi Mec\^anico team (VSSS-EMC) with an 84\% win
rate.

This thesis continues that line of work.  \textcite{brandao2022} showed
that 3v3 multi-agent learning is feasible, but the experiments there were
constrained by the cost of collecting enough useful experience.  The central
question addressed here is what becomes possible when training can both
generate more transitions per wall-clock second and make better use of the
situations it samples.

\section{Objective}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The team already has a sequence of works showing that learned policies can
perform meaningful 3v3 soccer behaviors.  However, training and evaluating
these policies remains expensive: the existing simulator architecture limits
how many interactions can be produced per unit time.  This cost is one of the main
practical barriers that separate \gls{rl} research in \gls{vsss} from routine
use inside the ITAndroids development cycle.

The objective of this work is to remove that barrier and to use the resulting capacity: to build a simulator and a training
suite that make large-scale \gls{rl} practical for 3v3 \gls{vsss}, to train a competitive team with them, and to evaluate which parts of the training
procedure, in particular an online curriculum over initial situations, matter.  The simulator is faster than the ODE-based ones because its 2D physics is simpler and because it runs batched on a \gls{gpu} (Section~\ref{sec:throughput_bottleneck}); its contact physics is
kept accurate where learning is sensitive to it.

\section{Literature Review}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

This section reviews the work most relevant to a learning-based approach to
3v3 \gls{vsss}.  It proceeds from the broad milestones that established deep
reinforcement learning as a viable tool for strategic control, through the
specific body of \gls{vsss} research developed within the ITAndroids team, to
accelerated simulation and sample efficiency improvements.

\subsection{Deep Reinforcement Learning in Games and Robotics}

The modern interest in deep reinforcement learning for sequential
decision-making was catalyzed by a sequence of results in games of increasing
complexity.  AlphaGo defeated a professional Go player by combining
deep neural networks with Monte Carlo tree search~\cite{silver2016mastering},
and its successor AlphaGo Zero reached superhuman play through self-play
alone, learning without any human game data~\cite{silver2017mastering}.  These
results established two ideas central to this work: that a single learned
function can encode sophisticated strategy, and that self-play can generate the
training signal needed to reach it.

Subsequent work scaled these ideas to real-time, multi-agent, partially
observable settings.  OpenAI Five coordinated a team of five agents to defeat
human champions at the video game Dota~2~\cite{berner2019dota}, and AlphaStar
reached Grandmaster level in StarCraft~II by training a league of continually
adapting agents, each representing a distinct strategy, to prevent the policy
from overfitting to a single style of play~\cite{starcraft_marl}.  The
league-training idea, that robustness requires facing a diverse
population of opponents and not only the latest policy, informs
the self-play design considered in this work.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning in IEEE VSSS}

Within the \gls{vsss} category specifically, a focused body of work, much of it
produced within the ITAndroids team, has progressively expanded the role of
reinforcement learning from isolated skills toward full-team play.

\textcite{medeiros2020} introduced reinforcement learning to \gls{vsss} strategy
by training a single robot, with \gls{ppo}, in a scenario where a support player must stop the ball heading to its side
in the presence of an opponent.  Two design choices from that
work recur in the subsequent literature.  First, the
action was a desired position on the field, converted into wheel commands by a navigation (line-following) controller, as in the 2019 monograph of the same author~\cite{tg_thiago}.  Second, because goal events are too sparse to
drive early learning, the reward was shaped with intermediate terms and training
was organized as a curriculum, with the agent and ball beginning close together
and gradually separating and the opponent initially stationary and later set in
motion.  Agents trained with this curriculum succeeded in $79.9\%$ of the episodes, against $72.1\%$ without it and $45.5\%$ for the
team's classical heuristic.

The 2019 monograph~\cite{tg_thiago} also broadened the study from a single skill to four scenarios (intercepting the ball on the
player's side and recovering it to the opponent's side, each with and without an
opponent).  The later master's thesis~\cite{master_thiago} switched the action to the desired wheel speeds (with a four-frame action repeat over $60$\,Hz physics, that is, $15$\,Hz),
trained two robots cooperating in an offensive move with a single centralized network that permutes the state between agents, and introduced
self-play; its author states that it is the first work to combine self-play with \gls{ppo} in \gls{vsss}.  Wheel-speed actions and shared networks are antecedents
of the shared-policy, self-play approach taken here.

\textcite{tg_Yamane} addressed a different role and a different challenge: a learned
goalkeeper policy and its transfer to physical
hardware.  While the earlier ITAndroids works evaluated policies only in
simulation, that work confronts the reality gap between the simulator and the
real robot, treating line-following control and decision-making under real
sensing and actuation noise.  Its treatment of sim-to-real transfer frames the
domain-randomization and noise-injection considerations that the present work
leaves to future work.

\textcite{master_thay} moved the framework into an explicitly adversarial,
multi-agent regime, studying penalty-kick scenarios in which an attacker and a
goalkeeper are trained against each other.  The central
question of that work is how to choose the opponent during self-play.  It
formalizes a family of opponent-sampling strategies that differ in how much of
the training history they draw from: sampling uniformly from all
past versions of the opponent (RPO-All), from only the most recent half (RPO-Half), or from the most recent quarter (RPO-Quarter).  It analyzes how this choice affects the quality
of the learned policy.  This is the design space of the self-play scheme of
Section~\ref{sec:selfplay}, and that analysis is the
most directly applicable guidance available for it.

Beyond the ITAndroids line, \textcite{bassani2020} developed an open
reinforcement-learning framework for \gls{vsss} and demonstrated sim-to-real
transfer of trained policies, with a resulting team placing competitively at the
2019 Latin American Robotics Competition.  Closest in spirit
to this thesis, \textcite{brandao2022} (Deep Learning Brazil and Pequi Mec\^anico, with ITA's LAB-SCA) trained a full 3v3 team with multi-agent
\gls{ppo} and self-play in MuJoCo, using $200$ environment instances on CPU only and a shared policy across the three homogeneous
robots, and reported a policy capable
of defeating the strongest heuristic opponent available to them.
That work establishes that full-team 3v3 learning is feasible; the present
work takes that feasibility as its starting point and asks what becomes possible
once the simulation-throughput and sample-efficiency constraints under which it
operated are relaxed.

\subsection{Accelerated Simulation for Reinforcement Learning}

On-policy algorithms such as \gls{ppo} consume a large volume of fresh
environment transitions, which makes simulation throughput a primary determinant
of training cost.  The \gls{vsss} works above run physics on the \gls{cpu} and
communicate with the learner across a process boundary, an architecture whose
throughput scales poorly with the number of parallel environments.

Two broad approaches have emerged to remove this bottleneck.  The first places
the physics engine directly on the \gls{gpu}: Isaac Gym~\cite{makoviychuk2021isaacgym}
and its successor Isaac Lab run a PhysX-based rigid-body simulator on the \gls{gpu},
exposing simulation state as tensors consumed by the policy without any \gls{cpu}
detour, though the stack is tied to Linux and NVIDIA hardware.
MJX~\cite{zakka2025mujocoplayground}, a just-in-time-compiled reimplementation
of MuJoCo~\cite{todorov2012mujoco}, brings that contact-rich physics engine into a batched regime.
Brax~\cite{freeman2021brax} implements a differentiable rigid-body engine
entirely in JAX.  In the robot soccer context,
\textcite{scaling_marl_2d} built a custom 2D environment whose physics consists
of simple vectorizable operations and reported throughput one to two orders of
magnitude higher than MuJoCo-backed alternatives, demonstrating that a
purpose-built batched environment reduces training time by avoiding the overhead
of a general engine.

The second approach keeps the full training loop on the same accelerator as the
simulator.  Stoix~\cite{toledo2024stoix} provides JAX-native, compiled
\gls{ppo} implementations, RLax~\cite{deepmind2020rlax} provides JAX building blocks for RL algorithms, and rsl-rl~\cite{rslrl} serves as the PyTorch companion
to the Isaac stack.

The simulator developed in this work follows this paradigm, applying batched
JAX physics to the \gls{vsss} domain.

\subsection{Prioritized Experience and Scenario Selection}

A complementary way to reduce training cost is to improve the value of each
transition rather than the rate at which transitions are produced.  Prioritized
Experience Replay~\cite{schaul2016per} established that, in off-policy learning,
replaying transitions in proportion to their temporal-difference error learns
faster than uniform replay. \gls{plr} extends this
intuition to the selection of \emph{which situations to play} rather than which
past transitions to revisit: it scores environment configurations by an estimate
of their future learning potential and biases the agent's experience toward the
configurations expected to be most informative, inducing an implicit curriculum
without any predefined ordering of difficulty~\cite{prioritized_level_replay}.  The curriculum
proposed in this work adapts this principle to \gls{vsss}, treating each
initialized game situation as a level, with a simpler priority derived from the returns of recent episodes.

The same selection problem also appears in multi-agent self-play.  AlphaStar's
league training, for example, did not train only against the latest policy; it
maintained a population of agents so that the learner faced varied opponents and
did not overfit to a single strategy~\cite{starcraft_marl}.  The curriculum of this work addresses the analogous question on the environment side: which game
situations should the current policy experience next?

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A JAX simulator for 3v3 \gls{vsss} with accurate contact physics} (Chapter~\ref{chap:sim}).  It steps thousands of games in parallel on one device, models ball spin, skidding and wall pop-ups,
        detects the earliest ball contact by swept tests, projects coupled contacts in a fixed number of iterations, and shares scoring, referee and restart rules among three environment interfaces.
        Its correctness is protected by a regression suite of collision failures found in earlier versions.
  \item \textbf{A fully on-device \gls{ppo} training suite} (Chapter~\ref{chap:sim}), with Brand\~ao-style observations, reward and shared policy, historical self-play, and a compiled generation that
        contains rollout, advantage estimation and optimization.
  \item \textbf{An online return-based restart curriculum} (Chapter~\ref{chap:training}) that replays initial situations according to the returns of recent episodes inside the compiled loop, evaluated by ablation
        against uniform restarts (Chapter~\ref{chap:results}).
\end{enumerate}
The curriculum is inspired by \gls{plr} (Section~\ref{sec:curriculum} states how it differs).
This work also extends the body of literature on applying deep \gls{rl} to full-team decision-making and control in the IEEE \gls{vsss} league.
