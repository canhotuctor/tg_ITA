This chapter defines the methodology: how the learning problem is posed on the simulator of
Chapter~\ref{chap:sim}.  It covers the decision problem, the policy networks, the symmetries that are exploited,
the self-play scheme, the online curriculum that chooses which initial situations to replay, and the protocol used to
evaluate the resulting policies.  The compiled training loop that executes this methodology is described in Section~\ref{sec:ppo}
of Chapter~\ref{chap:sim}.  The formulation and the policy
architecture follow \textcite{brandao2022}, the most recent
work on multi-robot self-play for VSSS developed with ITA's LAB-SCA, so the results are comparable
with that baseline; the curriculum and the compiled loop are
contributions of this work.

% ==================================================================
\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}
% ==================================================================

The learning agent controls the blue team (three robots) against a yellow opponent.
Time advances in decision steps of $0.1$\,s ($10$\,Hz); an episode lasts at most
$100$ steps ($10$\,s) and \emph{ends at the first goal}; \textcite{brandao2022} use $40$\,s episodes, and the shorter horizon is a choice of this work.  A goal
terminates the episode, while reaching the time limit truncates it: a truncated episode keeps its bootstrap
(the target is $r+\gamma V(s')$ with $\gamma=0.99$), whereas a terminated one has a zero bootstrap, so the last state is not treated as absorbing when only time ran out.  Each robot is an agent that observes locally and acts on its
own wheels, but all three share one set of network weights.

\subsection{Observation}

Every robot receives its own observation, expressed in a canonical frame in which its
team always attacks toward $+x$ (the yellow team's inputs are mirrored), so the same
policy can play either side.  One frame has $53$ features:
\begin{itemize}
  \item normalized positions of the seven entities (self, two allies, three opponents, ball), $14$;
  \item heading as $\sin\theta$, $\cos\theta$ and $\theta/\pi$, $3$;
  \item bearings (as $\sin$, $\cos$ and normalized angle) from the robot to its two allies and three opponents, and from the ball to both goals ($7$ angles), $21$;
  \item normalized distances from the robot to its two allies, three opponents and the ball, and from the ball to both goals, $8$;
  \item the previous left/right wheel commands of the three allies, $6$;
  \item the normalized time remaining, $1$.
\end{itemize}
The policy input stacks the last eight frames (a $0.8$\,s history) into a $424$-dimensional
vector.  Position and heading readings are corrupted with Gaussian noise whose standard
deviations ($1.85$\,mm and $1.68$\,mm in $x$ and $y$, and $0.031$\,rad in heading) are those that \textcite{brandao2022} report
for the real camera pipeline, so the policy is never trained on exact state.

\subsection{Action}

Each robot outputs two numbers in $[-1,1]$, the normalized left and right wheel
speeds (Section~\ref{sec:sim_physics}), at $10$\,Hz.  The simulator rate-limits them toward these targets
across the physics sub-steps of the decision step.

\subsection{Reward}
\label{sec:reward}

The reward is the Brand\~ao reward, Eqs.~(6)--(16) of~\cite{brandao2022}.  For robot $i$
it combines ball progress, proximity and two angular positioning terms,
\begin{equation}
    r_i = 0.7\,r_{\mathrm{speed}} + 0.1\,r_{\mathrm{prox}} + 0.1\,r^{\mathrm{off}}_i + 0.1\,r^{\mathrm{def}}_i ,
\end{equation}
where $r_{\mathrm{speed}} = \mathrm{clip}\big((d_{k-1}-d_k-0.05)/0.14,\,-1,\,1\big)$ is the
clipped reduction of the ball's distance $d$ to the opponent goal over one step (with a
$5$\,cm dead zone), $r_{\mathrm{prox}} = -\min(d_{\mathrm{rb}},\,1\,\mathrm{m})$ with $d_{\mathrm{rb}}$ the distance from the nearest blue robot to the ball, and the offensive and defensive angle terms
are $\angle/\pi - 1\in[-1,0]$, where the angles are those at the ball between the robot and the
opponent goal and at the robot between the ball and the own goal, respectively.  The speed and
proximity terms are shared by the team; the angle terms are per-robot.  When a goal is scored the
continuous reward is replaced by
\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{goal}} = 10\,g\left(1 + \frac{H - k}{H}\right),
\end{equation}
with $g=+1$ for a blue goal, $-1$ for a yellow goal, horizon $H = 100$ (the episode limit) and step $k$, so earlier goals pay up to
$20$ and conceding costs the same.  Rewards are per agent, and each robot's value function estimates its own return.

\section{Policy Architecture}
\label{sec:policy}
% ==================================================================

\paragraph{Shared actor.}
The actor is a multilayer perceptron with tanh activations ($424\to300\to200\to100$) applied to the
observation of one robot.  Because the three robots use the same weights, the batch of three robot
observations is processed as three samples and the team behaviour arises from the
shared parameters acting on different local views.  The head outputs two positive parameters per wheel,
$\alpha,\beta\ge 1$ (a softplus plus one), of a Beta distribution on $[0,1]$ that is rescaled to $[-1,1]$.  The Beta
distribution has bounded support, which matches the actuator limits without clipping a Gaussian, and
the deterministic policy used at evaluation time is its mean, $2\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)-1$.

\paragraph{Critic.}
The value function has its own network ($424\to256\to128\to64\to1$), not shared with the actor, so
policy and value gradients do not interfere.  The policy is the shared per-robot actor above, as in~\cite{brandao2022}.

\paragraph{Mirror augmentation (used in all reported runs).}
The field is symmetric about its long axis ($y\to-y$): reflecting an observation (negating the $y$ coordinates and the signed angular components, and swapping each robot's previous
left/right wheel commands) must yield the mirrored action, that is, the two wheel components exchanged.  At every minibatch the loss is computed on both the original samples and their reflections, and the two gradients are averaged.  The
old log-probabilities, values and bootstrap values that the reflected loss needs are computed on the reflected observations during the rollout, and the advantages and targets are computed for the reflected trajectory as well.  This is a
data-augmentation signal that doubles the effective data per update at the cost of a second forward and backward pass.  \emph{All reported runs, in both curriculum conditions, use this augmentation}; no run without it is reported, so its effect is not isolated by the experiments.

\section{Exploiting Symmetries}
\label{sec:symmetries}
% ==================================================================

The three symmetries of the game introduced in Section~\ref{sec:symmetry_theory} are used.  \emph{Permutation symmetry} is exploited by the shared policy: one network
controls the three robots, and all three contribute transitions to its update.  \emph{Side symmetry} is exploited by the canonical frame of the observation
(Section~\ref{sec:formulation}): each team is observed as if attacking toward $+x$, so one policy plays either side and the same weights serve as learner and as opponent.
\emph{Reflection} about the longitudinal axis is exploited by the mirror augmentation of Section~\ref{sec:policy}, which is on in all reported runs.  The cost of parameter sharing, a pressure toward a common behaviour that limits
role specialization, is revisited in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}.

\section{Self-Play}
\label{sec:selfplay}
% ==================================================================

The opponent is always a frozen earlier copy of the learner, so the training signal is a moving
but never-collapsing curriculum of difficulty (the opponent improves only when the learner has surpassed it),
a practice common to multi-agent game training~\cite{silver2017mastering,berner2019dota}.
Following the Brand\~ao scheme, each training run holds \emph{one} opponent snapshot at a time.  The opponent starts as the
untrained initial policy.  Every $10$ generations the current policy is evaluated against the current
opponent in a batch of $100$ matches of $120$\,s (fixed seeds, deterministic mean actions), and snapshots are archived every $S=50$ generations or when they replace the opponent.  The opponent is replaced by the
candidate when the mean goal difference per match reaches a threshold,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\big(g^{\text{blue}}_m - g^{\text{yellow}}_m\big) \ge 0.6 ,
\end{equation}
and kept otherwise.  The value $0.6$ means the learner must clearly dominate, scoring on average more than half a goal per match more than it concedes, before the target moves, which keeps the opponent stable long enough to learn against.

The suite also supports a larger \emph{pool} of snapshots from which each reset draws an opponent according to a mixture of
selectors (uniform over all history, over the most recent half, the latest only, plus random and stationary baselines); the experiments of this work use the single-opponent scheme only.

\section{Online Return-Based Curriculum}
\label{sec:curriculum}
% ==================================================================

\paragraph{Motivation.}
With the simulator's random initialization (robots and ball placed uniformly at random) the situations vary widely in
usefulness: some are decided instantly, and some are chaotic.  The preliminary report (TG-1) found that training on kickoff alone learns but covers little of the state
space, whereas flat random initialization learned poorly under a sparse reward.  Prioritized level
replay~\cite{prioritized_level_replay} addresses exactly this by replaying levels in proportion to a learning-potential score.
The method below is a simplified, fully on-device variant of that idea.  It differs from PLR in that the score is derived from episode returns, not value-prediction error,
and the buffer is refreshed every generation and does not persist.

\paragraph{Levels.}
An initial situation is fully determined by a pseudo-random key, since the random reset maps a key to robot poses
and ball position deterministically.  A \emph{level} is therefore stored as its two-word key, and replaying it regenerates the same initial placement.
The opponent policy, the policy's sampling noise and the camera noise are drawn afresh, so a replay revisits the situation, not the exact trajectory.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
At the end of each generation, every episode that completed during the rollout contributes its level key and its return
$G$ (averaged over the three robots).  Returns are min--max normalized over that batch,
$u = (G - G_{\min})/(G_{\max}-G_{\min})$, and the score is
\begin{equation}
    w = u\,(1-u) .
\end{equation}
The score is zero for the best and worst episodes of the batch and maximal for episodes near the middle of the
return range.  The intuition is a learnability heuristic, following \textcite{rutherford2024noregrets}, who propose to train on levels that the agent can sometimes solve but not always: situations that are always won or always lost carry little
gradient, whereas intermediate outcomes mark situations where the current policy is neither mastering nor failing.  Scoring by learnability in that sense requires an estimate of the success rate on each candidate level, which means evaluating the policy on a large set of levels before choosing among them.
That extra evaluation would cost a large share of the budget that the fast simulator is meant to save.  The score used here therefore takes the return of an episode that was played anyway as a free proxy for that estimate:
after min--max normalization, $u$ plays the role of a success probability and $u(1-u)$ that of learnability.  This is the simplest prioritized curriculum that fits inside the compiled loop, at the cost of noisy scores (one episode, one stochastic return).  The
proxy is assumed, not shown to work; the ablation of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} tests only whether the resulting training is better.

\paragraph{Buffer and sampling.}
A random subset of at most $K=1024$ completed episodes of the latest rollout forms the replay buffer, replacing its previous contents.
When an environment resets, with probability $\rho$ (the \emph{replay fraction}) it draws a level from the buffer with
probability proportional to $w + 10^{-3}$; otherwise (or while the buffer is empty) it starts a fresh random level.  Everything
runs inside the compiled generation, so the curriculum adds no host communication.  The configuration used here is $\rho = 0.25$, $K=1024$.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Run the rollout; every reset draws a level from the buffer with probability $\rho$ or a fresh key otherwise.
  \item Collect each completed episode's key and mean return.
  \item Normalize the returns, compute $w = u(1-u)$, sample up to $K$ episodes at random.
  \item Overwrite the buffer with these keys and scores.
  \item Update the policy with PPO.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Scores are one stochastic sample of the return, so they are noisy; the buffer has no memory beyond one generation and no staleness handling; the score is relative to the
current batch and not comparable across generations; and the requirement that levels be reproducible from a key restricts the method to the random-initialization mode.  These are
simplifications chosen for an implementation that fits inside a single compiled program.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}
% ==================================================================

Two kinds of evidence are used.  The quantitative evaluation below gives win rates and goal differences against fixed opponents, but it is a coarse signal in this problem: a policy can improve in ways that a win rate against a stationary, random or earlier opponent
does not resolve, and the ITAndroids classical strategy may well beat the learned team.  The main evidence of what training produces is therefore \emph{qualitative}: the behaviours that appear as generations advance.  These are observed by replaying snapshots of a lineage at chosen generations on fixed seeds, with trajectories,
ball and robot position heat maps, and annotated frame sequences (Section~\ref{sec:behaviour}).  Qualitative observation is not a measurement, so it is reported as the author's reading of those replays, supported by the figures and by the quantitative
curves, and not as a statistical claim.

Training returns are not a fair measure of strength, since they depend on the curriculum and on the moving opponent.  Policies are therefore compared with a
separate evaluator that uses fixed seeds and the deterministic mean policy, plays complete matches, and reports goals for and against.  Matches should be played both from the standard kickoff and from random
placements, because the evaluator resets with the experiment's own initialization mode (random in the curriculum runs).  The reported
metrics are win rate, draw rate, goal difference per match, and goals scored per match, each with a bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval over $n$ matches.  % TODO: choose n per opponent.
The opponents are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the stationary and random baseline opponents of the simulator, as sanity checks;
  \item earlier snapshots of the same run, to measure progress over training;
  \item snapshots from runs with and without the curriculum, for the head-to-head comparison;
  \item the ITAndroids classical strategy stack, run in the external Travesim simulator~\cite{travesim}, which fixes a reference not made by the same learning process; only the last trained policy of each lineage plays against it (Section~\ref{sec:itandroids_eval}).
\end{itemize}
For the classical-strategy comparison the Travesim physics differs from this simulator's, so the result measures transfer between simulators in
addition to playing strength, and this is stated in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\subsection{Protocol Against the ITAndroids Classical Strategy}
\label{sec:itandroids_eval}

The classical strategy plays in Travesim~\cite{travesim}, a Webots-based simulator, through a bridge: a Python harness loads a checkpoint, rebuilds the eight-frame observation history, runs inference at $10$\,Hz and converts the
policy's commands into Travesim wheel commands, while the ITAndroids agent runs headless in its simulator-only mode with Travesim's robot geometry.  Vision, team commands and referee messages travel as protobuf over UDP.  Scoring, fouls, placements
and kickoffs are handled by a harness that reuses this simulator's \texttt{MatchRules}, and not by the official ITAndroids referee.  In a pilot of this protocol, each checkpoint played $10$ matches of $120$\,s against the \texttt{EXTREME\_OFFENSIVE} strategy, in five paired seeds with the policy
playing five matches as each color; all matches were logged with checkpoint hashes.  The same protocol is applied here, only to the last policy of each lineage, because this code base is not easily parallelized.  
Its limits are those of the pilot: the referee and restart behaviour are simplified, the two systems run at different control rates, and the ITAndroids agent can request wheel speeds above Travesim's $68$\,rad/s limit, which are then clipped.  The result is therefore a comparison of a reference strength, not a tournament-grade ranking.
