This chapter describes the simulator that produces the experience used in the
rest of this work.  The design goal is a VSSS 3v3 environment that is
(i)~fast enough to run thousands of matches in parallel on one accelerator,
(ii)~physically accurate where learning is sensitive to physics, namely the contacts
among ball, robots and walls, and (iii)~rule-consistent, so that goals,
kickoffs and referee stoppages mean the same thing in every interface.
Section~\ref{sec:sim_arch} gives the architecture, Section~\ref{sec:sim_physics}
the physics, Section~\ref{sec:sim_rules} the match rules, and
Sections~\ref{sec:sim_validation} and~\ref{sec:sim_throughput} the validation and
throughput.  Section~\ref{sec:ppo} then describes the training suite built on top of it, in which rollout, advantage estimation and
optimization run as one compiled program on the same device.  This chapter is the engineering core of the work: it supersedes the simpler simulator and the
centralized training baseline of the preliminary report (TG-1), whose physics model and training loop were far less complete.

\section{Design and Architecture}
\label{sec:sim_arch}
% ==================================================================

\paragraph{Functional state.}
The simulation state is an immutable JAX pytree and a step is a pure function
$s_{k+1} = \mathrm{step}(s_k, a_k)$, which allows it to be compiled with
\texttt{jit}, batched with \texttt{vmap} and rolled out with \texttt{lax.scan}
without leaving the accelerator~\cite{jax2018github}.  All physics arrays are
\texttt{float32}.  Table~\ref{tab:sim_state} lists the state fields.

\paragraph{Three front-ends, one set of rules.}
The physics step is wrapped by three environment front-ends
(Figure~\ref{fig:sim_arch}).  \texttt{VSSEnv} is a single-match Gymnasium
environment with a Pygame~\cite{pygame2024} renderer and Python opponent callables;
\texttt{VSSVecEnv} is a batched Gymnasium \texttt{VectorEnv} for external
clients; and \texttt{VSSJumanjiEnv} is a pure functional environment in the
style of Jumanji~\cite{bonnet2023jumanji} used by the native PPO trainer of
Chapter~\ref{chap:training}.  All three delegate scoring, referee decisions,
restart placement, rewards and observations to one shared module
(\texttt{match\_rules}), so a behaviour change in the rules cannot make training
and evaluation diverge.

\paragraph{Time base.}
The policy acts at $10$\,Hz (control step $\Delta t = 0.1$\,s).  Each control
step runs $6$ physics sub-steps, so the physics runs at $60$\,Hz with
$\delta t = 1/60$\,s.  When a goal occurs inside a control step the remaining
sub-steps are skipped (the state is frozen with \texttt{lax.cond}) so the event
is reported exactly once.  The experiments use this $10$\,Hz control rate with $6$ sub-steps.

\subsection{Geometry and Parameters}

The field is the 150\,cm $\times$ 130\,cm playing surface of the 3v3 category with
40\,cm wide, 10\,cm deep goals, 7\,cm corner chamfers and the goal-area
geometry of the rulebook (Chapter~\ref{chap:vsss}).  Robots are $7.5$\,cm squares of mass
$0.312$\,kg; the ball is a golf ball ($r = 21.35$\,mm, $m = 46$\,g).
Table~\ref{tab:sim_params} collects the physical parameters, with the origin of

\subsection{Actuation}

The policy outputs normalized wheel commands $u_L,u_R\in[-1,1]$ per robot.  They
set target wheel speeds $w^\star = u\,v_{\max}$, with
$v_{\max} = 1.30$\,m/s.  The applied wheel speed moves toward the target by at
most $a_{\max}\delta t$ per sub-step,
\begin{equation}
    w \leftarrow w + \mathrm{clip}\!\left(w^\star - w,\; -a_{\max}\delta t,\; a_{\max}\delta t\right),
    \qquad a_{\max} = 1\,g,
\end{equation}
which stands in for motor torque limits and removes impossible instantaneous
velocity changes.  In the default \emph{kinematic} drive model the body velocity is then
overwritten from the wheel speeds,
\begin{equation}
    v = \tfrac12 (w_L + w_R), \qquad
    \omega = \frac{w_R - w_L}{L}, \qquad
    (\dot x,\dot y) = v(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),
\end{equation}
with wheelbase $L = 53$\,mm.  Because velocity is overwritten at every sub-step,
a robot driven into an obstacle is simply stopped by the contact solver and
carries no momentum between sub-steps.

An opt-in \emph{dynamic} drive model keeps the robot velocity as state.  Each
wheel acts as a DC motor whose force is proportional to the wheel-speed error,
saturated at the stall force $\tau_{\max}/r_w$ and at the traction limit
$\mu g\,m/2$; sideways velocity decays only by wheel friction.  Stall, pushing
contests between robots and momentum exchange then emerge from the physics
instead of being imposed.  The dynamic model is validated by unit tests
(Section~\ref{sec:sim_validation}) but is \emph{not} used in the training
experiments of this thesis, which use the kinematic default.  % TODO: confirm.

\subsection{Ball Dynamics: Skidding, Rolling, Spin and Flight}

The ball carries a three-dimensional spin and a height above the floor, which
lets the model reproduce effects that matter to the game even though the field is
planar: a struck ball first \emph{skids}, then \emph{rolls}, and a ball that
is hit while rolling can pop up off a wall.

\paragraph{Floor contact.}
At the contact point the slip velocity is
$\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v} + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(-r\hat{\mathbf n}) - \mathbf{v}_s$,
projected on the contact plane, where $\mathbf v_s$ is the surface velocity.
Coulomb friction opposes slip and, per unit mass, supplies a tangential impulse
\begin{equation}
    p = \min\!\left(\mu\,J_n,\; \frac{\lVert\mathbf u\rVert}{1 + 1/k}\right),
    \label{eq:friction_impulse}
\end{equation}
where $J_n$ is the normal impulse per unit mass and $k$ the ball's inertia factor
($I = k\,m\,r^2$).  The second term is the impulse that exactly stops slip, so
friction never adds energy.  A ball launched without spin therefore skids until it
rolls at $1/(1+k) = 5/7$ of its launch speed (Figure~\ref{fig:ball_floor}), a classical
result that is also one of the unit tests.  Once rolling, the ball decelerates at $\mu_r g$ and its spin
follows its velocity; spin about the vertical axis decays at a constant rate.

\input{Cap5/figs/fig_ball_floor}

\paragraph{Flight.}
A ball given vertical velocity follows a ballistic arc under gravity and bounces
with restitution $e = 0.75$ on landing; bounces too small to outlast a sub-step
are settled.  This is only used for the small pop-ups produced by wall friction
on a rolling ball; the game remains planar.

\paragraph{Face impulses.}
When a vertical face (a wall, post or robot side) strikes the ball with approach
speed $v_n<0$ along the face normal, the normal impulse per unit ball mass is
\begin{equation}
    J_n = -(1+e)\,\frac{m_f}{m_b + m_f}\,v_n ,
\end{equation}
where the mass ratio is $1$ for a fixed wall and uses the robot mass for a robot.
Equation~\eqref{eq:friction_impulse} then gives the friction impulse, so a
glancing hit converts tangential speed into spin and a rolling ball hitting a
wall receives a small upward kick (Figure~\ref{fig:face_impulse}).  The reaction is applied to the robot.

\input{Cap5/figs/fig_face_impulse}

\subsection{Contact Resolution}
\label{sec:sim_contact}

Contact handling is where most of the engineering effort went, because errors there
are not cosmetic: a ball that tunnels through a robot or a goal post, or is squeezed
outside the field, corrupts both the reward signal and the referee decisions.  An
earlier version of the simulator resolved each sub-step by integrating all bodies and
then fixing overlaps by summing positional corrections.  Under high ball speeds or
crowded robots, a later correction could push a body back through a wall that an
earlier correction had just respected, and a fast ball could cross a robot or the
goal front completely between two samples.  The current design separates
\emph{detecting} the first contact in time from \emph{making the final geometry
consistent}.

\paragraph{Overlap measures.}
Both the discrete impulses and the projection need a normal and a penetration depth for
each pair in contact (Figure~\ref{fig:contact_geometry}).  For the ball and a robot, the ball
centre is expressed in the robot frame and clamped to the square, which gives the robot's
closest point to the ball.  Robot pairs use the separating-axis test over the four face
normals of the two squares and keep the axis of least overlap.  A robot is kept inside the
field by clamping its centre to at least its support distance $h(\lvert\cos\theta\rvert+\lvert\sin\theta\rvert)$
from each wall (and the analogous support along each chamfer diagonal), zeroing the outward
velocity; it may enter a goal pocket only if its whole footprint fits between the posts.

\input{Cap5/figs/fig_contact_geometry}

\paragraph{Swept ball motion.}
The field boundary is a fixed set of oriented segments (walls, chamfers, goal posts
and pocket rails).  For a ball centre moving as $\mathbf p(t) = \mathbf p_0 + t\,\mathbf d$,
$t\in[0,1]$ within a sub-step, a finite segment expanded by the ball radius is a
capsule (Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}a), so candidate hit times come from two cases: the face case, a linear equation
\begin{equation}
    t = \frac{\pm r - \mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf p_0 - \mathbf a)}{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf d},
\end{equation}
accepted only if the contact point lies within the segment, and the endpoint case, a
quadratic $\lVert \mathbf p_0 + t\mathbf d - \mathbf v\rVert^2 = r^2$ for each end vertex
$\mathbf v$.  Receding or parallel candidates are rejected so a ball at rest on a wall does
not generate zero-time events.  Robots are cast the same way, in the robot frame: the
ball's relative path is tested against the robot's square, with the robot's rotation
approximated by interpolating its orientation over two chords per sub-step so that a
spinning robot can strike a resting ball (Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}b).  Goal events are a third candidate class: the
ball scores only when its \emph{whole} body has crossed the goal line inside the mouth, per
the rulebook, and a wall or post hit earlier in the sub-step takes precedence (Figure~\ref{fig:events}a).

The simulator takes the earliest valid candidate, applies the face impulse of the
previous section at that instant, and continues with the remaining time (Figure~\ref{fig:events}b).  A bounded loop
of at most four events per sub-step keeps shapes static for XLA; if the budget is exhausted
the residual travel is discarded.  The bounded dynamic loop prevents reverse-mode
differentiation through the step, which is acceptable because the learner does not
differentiate through the simulator.

\input{Cap5/figs/fig_sweep}
\input{Cap5/figs/fig_events}

\paragraph{Discrete impulses.}
Stage~4 handles contacts that the ball sweep does not, such as two robots meeting or a robot
pressing a ball it already touches.  With the overlap measures above, the ball receives the face
impulse of the previous section (mass share $m_r/(m_b+m_r)$, surface velocity including the robot's
spin) and robot pairs exchange an inelastic impulse with $e=0.2$ along the separating axis, as
equal masses.  Only the velocity changes of this stage are kept; its positional corrections are
discarded and left to the projection.

\paragraph{Coupled projection.}
After the discrete impulses, a fixed number of position-only iterations (six) repairs the
remaining overlaps without adding any further impulses (Figure~\ref{fig:projection}).  Ball corrections are averaged over
simultaneous contacts and clipped at the first static wall, so projection stops at the first wall contact instead of pushing
the ball through it.  Robot--robot overlaps are relaxed Gauss--Seidel style: the complete
graph on six robots decomposes into five perfect matchings of three disjoint pairs, so each
round corrects three independent pairs in parallel (a fixed, vectorizable schedule).  After
every round the robots are re-clamped to the field, and a robot that the clamp had to move is
treated as immovable in the next round so that its partner absorbs the whole separation, in the
spirit of position-based dynamics~\cite{muller2007pbd}.  Because the iteration budget is fixed,
residual penetration remains possible in dense jams; Section~\ref{sec:sim_validation} quantifies it.

\input{Cap5/figs/fig_projection}

\subsection{Design Choices and Known Simplifications}

The model is a 2D rigid-body model with the following limitations, which
bound what conclusions the later chapters can draw:
\begin{itemize}
  \item robots are squares with equal positional weighting in the projection step, a numerical
        preconditioner rather than a physical mass model;
  \item rotating-robot paths are swept with two chords, and trajectory changes caused by
        impacts within a sub-step are not swept;
  \item the event budget of four per sub-step and the six projection iterations are fixed;
  \item Gaussian position and heading noise is the only sensing imperfection modelled; there is no communication latency, camera delay or battery effect, so
        policies are not yet validated against real robots;
  \item several coefficients are estimates (Table~\ref{tab:sim_params}), and no physical
        calibration against real ball trajectories has been performed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Match Rules and Referee}
\label{sec:sim_rules}
% ==================================================================

Match semantics live in a single module shared by all front-ends.  After each control step the
module receives the pre-step and post-physics states, the goal event, and the step counter, and
returns the carried state, observation, reward, referee state and any restart.  The referee
implements the RoboCore VSSS rulebook (version~3.0, 2025)~\cite{robocore_vsss_rules} for the
calls that can be inferred from the 2D state: goals; attacker and defender goal-area violations;
goalkeeper possession timeout; goal-area and field impasses; personal fouls and goalkeeper
blocking; and a ball stopped inside a goal area.  Restarts are kickoff, penalty kick, goal kick,
free ball and free kick, and the match enters a stopped phase during which actions are zeroed
until the restart formation is placed.  Three referee modes are available:
\texttt{OFF} (goals only), \texttt{SIMPLIFIED} (stuck-ball free balls and the two-robot goal-area
fouls) and \texttt{FULL}.  The experiments of this work use the \texttt{SIMPLIFIED} mode.

Two rules matter especially for learning.  First, a goal requires the entire ball to cross
the line within the mouth, and the physics stops the remaining sub-steps so the event is scored once.
Second, \texttt{terminate\_on\_goal} makes an episode end at a goal, which is the default for the Brand\~ao-style policies of
Chapter~\ref{chap:training}~\cite{brandao2022}; otherwise a goal triggers a kickoff and play continues until
the time limit.

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:sim_validation}
% ==================================================================

Validation has two layers: automated tests that each pin down one physical or rule property, and
quantitative stress benchmarks.  No comparison to physical robot data has been made, so ``validation''
here means \emph{internal consistency and regression protection}, not fidelity to the real game.

\paragraph{Property tests.}
The test suite is grouped by what it protects:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Ball dynamics:} a resting ball stays at rest; a kicked ball skids and then rolls at $5/7$ of
        its speed; a rolling ball decelerates only by rolling resistance; backspin conserves angular
        momentum about the contact; a rolling ball pops up off a wall and settles while a skidding one does
        not; glancing hits convert sideways speed into spin; and ball contacts never add energy.
  \item \emph{Collision regressions:} each reproduces a failure of the earlier simulator.  A fast ball
        beside the goal bounces without a false goal; a ball fully crossing the open goal is scored; a robot
        pressing the ball against a side wall, a robot pushed by another robot, and a train of robots pushed
        into a wall all stay inside the field; two robots cannot drive into the ball from opposite sides;
        a fast ball cannot pass through a stationary robot; a spinning robot corner kicks a resting ball without
        penetration; and the robot-pair projection converges for a crowd in open field.
  \item \emph{Dynamic drive:} acceleration is limited by traction and saturates at top speed; a robot driving
        into a wall stalls and stays inside; momentum transfers between robots; equal head-on pushers deadlock
        while a stronger command wins; and contact creates no energy.
  \item \emph{Batching and parity:} a batched step equals the individual steps for the collision scenarios,
        the batched Jumanji step distinguishes a post/wall hit from a valid goal, and CPU and GPU steps agree.
  \item \emph{Rules:} referee, restart and kickoff behaviour is tested for the scalar, batched and Jumanji
        environments, including symmetry of the two teams' perspective.
\end{itemize}

\section{Fully Compiled PPO}
\label{sec:ppo}
% ==================================================================

The learner is Proximal Policy Optimization~\cite{schulman2017ppo} with generalized advantage
estimation~\cite{schulman2015gae}, implemented directly in JAX/Flax/Optax rather than through an external
library.  The motivation is the throughput bottleneck of the preliminary design (TG-1), where every step crossed the
host--device boundary into a CPU learner.  Here the entire generation is one compiled function:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Rollout.} An outer \texttt{lax.scan} of $T$ steps runs, for $N$ environments batched with \texttt{vmap}: policy inference, sampling, the opponent's action, the
        environment step and an automatic reset (\texttt{lax.cond}) of every environment whose episode ended.  Because
        consecutive eight-frame histories share seven frames, only the newest frame is stored and the histories are rebuilt
        for each optimization minibatch, which reduces rollout memory roughly eightfold.
  \item \textbf{Advantages.} GAE($\gamma,\lambda$) is computed by a reverse scan, with the value of the last state as
        bootstrap for truncated episodes and zero for terminated ones.  Advantages are normalized over the batch.
  \item \textbf{Update.} $E$ epochs over $M$ shuffled minibatches minimize the clipped surrogate
        $-\min\!\big(r_t A,\ \mathrm{clip}(r_t,1\!-\!\epsilon,1\!+\!\epsilon)A\big)$ together with a clipped value loss and an entropy bonus,
        followed by gradient-norm clipping and Adam.
\end{enumerate}
Shapes are static, so XLA compiles several generations into a single program and Python only intervenes to log
metrics and write snapshots.  Table~\ref{tab:ppo_hparams} lists the hyperparameters.
