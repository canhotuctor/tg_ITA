This chapter reports the results achieved in TG-1 and outlines the results
planned for TG-2.  Because the central TG-1 deliverable is the simulator
infrastructure rather than a trained policy, the preliminary results focus on
the simulator's measured throughput, its interface and architecture, and the
status of the prioritized-level-replay sampler.  The planned results describe
the experiments that this infrastructure was built to enable.

% ==================================================================
\section{Preliminary Results}
% ==================================================================

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Simulator Throughput}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

Throughput is reported as environment steps per second as a function of the
execution path and the number of parallel environments.  All measurements are taken after \gls{jit} warm-up (the initial call to
a \texttt{jit}-compiled function that triggers XLA ahead-of-time compilation,
followed by a short sequence of additional calls to drain JAX's asynchronous
execution queue), so that this one-time compilation cost is excluded from the
steady-state throughput figures.  Two hardware configurations were benchmarked,
reflecting the dual development and training setup described in Chapter~4: a
MacBook with an Apple M4 Pro chip, used for CPU execution during development,
and an Ubuntu~22.04 workstation with an NVIDIA RTX~3060 GPU, used for
accelerated experiments.

\subsubsection{Physics Throughput}

The first benchmark isolates the simulator physics from the learning system.
This measurement uses the raw JAX stepping kernel
($\mathrm{jit}(\mathrm{vmap}(\texttt{step}))$), without Gymnasium wrapping,
policy inference, rollout storage, or gradient updates.  No directly comparable
baseline is available in prior VSSS work, because the ODE-based simulators
discussed in Chapter~2 report training duration rather than raw physics-kernel
throughput.  This benchmark therefore measures the throughput ceiling achievable by
expressing the VSSS dynamics as batched array operations.

Table~\ref{tab:throughput_physics} reports the full measured raw physics
throughput on the Apple~M4~Pro as the number of parallel environments is swept
from $1$ to $1024$.  Throughput is computed as
$\text{steps/s} = n_{\mathrm{envs}} n_{\mathrm{steps}} / T$, where $T$ is the
total benchmark latency.  The latency columns report the corresponding
per-call latency of one batched simulator step, i.e., $T/n_{\mathrm{steps}}$,
summarized by its trimmed mean and standard deviation, its median, and its
95th percentile.  The throughput rises steeply with batch size and then
saturates near $3.1 \times 10^{5}$ steps per second once the kernel is
compute-bound: roughly $3.0 \times 10^{5}$ steps per second is already reached
at $256$ parallel environments.  The JIT compilation cost is a one-time
approximately 0.2\,s and is excluded from the steady-state numbers.  On the
RTX~3060, the same batched execution model scales to far larger batches and
reaches a peak of $5.4 \times 10^{6}$ steps per second at $4096$
parallel environments, more than an order of magnitude above the CPU ceiling.
Beyond that batch size the throughput recedes (to
$4.0 \times 10^{6}$ at $65536$) as the working set saturates the GPU's
memory bandwidth.  Table~\ref{tab:throughput_physics_rtx} reports the full
sweep up to $65536$ parallel environments.

The physics-only numbers validate the main simulator design choice: for this
domain, the relevant rigid-body kinematics, collisions, and friction model can
be evaluated as a large batch of array operations.  The resulting throughput is
well above the control frequency required by any single VSSS match; the value of
the approach comes from using that headroom to collect experience from hundreds
or thousands of independent games at once.  Figure~\ref{fig:throughput_latency}
shows the step latency of the raw kernel and the \texttt{VSSVecEnv} wrapper
across the full batch sweep on both platforms.

\subsubsection{Training Throughput}

The second benchmark measures the simulator as used by the current training
stack.  This path includes the Gymnasium-compatible \texttt{VSSVecEnv} wrapper
and, for the end-to-end measurement, a full Stable-Baselines3 \gls{ppo} cycle
with rollout collection and gradient updates.  Unlike the raw physics benchmark,
this result can be compared to the ODE-based training baseline derived in
Chapter~2, Table~\ref{tab:legacy_throughput}, because both quantities describe
environment steps delivered to an RL training loop.

Table~\ref{tab:throughput_wrapper} reports the throughput of the
\texttt{VSSVecEnv} wrapper alone (the execution path that an RL library
actually drives) across the same batch sweep.  Unlike the raw kernel, each
step here crosses the Python and Gymnasium boundary and converts between JAX
and NumPy arrays, so the wrapper recovers only a fraction of the physics
ceiling: about $5\%$ at a single environment, rising to $79\%$ at $1024$
environments as the fixed per-step cost is amortized.  At $256$ environments
the wrapper sustains $1.6 \times 10^{5}$ steps per second.

On the RTX~3060 the same wrapper path is strongly host-bound.  Each step incurs
a near-constant 5.4\,ms penalty for the round trip between the
device-resident JAX state and the NumPy arrays handed to the learner, which is
an order of magnitude larger than the CPU wrapper's overhead and dwarfs the
sub-millisecond GPU kernel at every batch size below $4096$.  As a result the
wrapper recovers only about $6\%$ of the GPU physics ceiling up to $1024$
environments, and even at $65536$ environments (where it sustains its peak
of $2.4 \times 10^{6}$ steps per second) it reaches only $59\%$ of the

The source of the gap is isolated in Table~\ref{tab:wrapper_overhead}, which
subtracts the median raw-kernel step latency from the median wrapper step
latency at each batch size.  The per-step overhead is roughly constant at
$0.6$--$0.9$\,ms regardless of batch size: it therefore dominates the small
batches, where it inflates a $30\,\mu$s kernel step by more than twenty-fold,
and is amortized as the batch grows and the device-side physics cost catches
up with it.  The mild upward drift of the overhead at the largest batches
tracks the size of the observation array marshalled back to the learner, which
grows linearly with the number of environments.

Table~\ref{tab:throughput_training} collects the headline numbers for direct
comparison against the ODE baselines.  This comparison is not strictly controlled: the ODE-based simulators implement
more elaborate collision physics (rigid-body dynamics via an ODE solver), while
the new simulator uses a simplified 2D model designed for array-level throughput
rather than physical fidelity (Section~\ref{sec:physics_model}, Chapter~4).
Part of the throughput advantage therefore reflects reduced per-step computation,
in addition to batching and GPU acceleration.  With this caveat, the comparison
separates two further effects.
First, even the current wrapped environment delivers orders of magnitude more
environment steps per second than the prior single-instance ODE training setup: about
$1.6 \times 10^{5}$ steps per second on the M4 Pro wrapper path and up to
$2.4 \times 10^{6}$ on the RTX~3060 wrapper path at the largest batch, compared
with 63--94 steps per second per simulator instance in
the legacy systems.  Second, when the full \gls{ppo} cycle is included,
end-to-end throughput depends strongly on the number of parallel environments
and the PPO minibatch size (the number of transitions processed per gradient
step during the update phase. Referred to as the \texttt{batch\_size} in the SB3 documentation \cite{raffin2021sb3}): as detailed in Tables~\ref{tab:ppo_pergen}
and~\ref{tab:ppo_pergen_rtx}, on the M4 Pro it ranges from
$1.1 \times 10^{3}$ steps per second for a single environment up to
$4.9 \times 10^{4}$ steps per second at $256$ environments with the
largest minibatch.  The RTX~3060 is slower at every batch size the M4 Pro was
tested at, because the SB3 learner forces a host round trip on every step that
masks the GPU's physics advantage; only by pushing to $65536$ environments
does it reach a peak of $5.8 \times 10^{4}$ steps per second, modestly
above the M4 Pro.  Even the fastest of these configurations is still well above the ODE-based
ITAndroids baseline (Section~\ref{sec:throughput_bottleneck}, Chapter~2) by two to three orders of
magnitude, yet remains almost two orders of magnitude below the raw simulator ceiling.

Table~\ref{tab:ppo_pergen} breaks the end-to-end cost down per generation,
separating the rollout phase (environment stepping and policy inference) from
the PPO update phase (PyTorch gradient steps on CPU).  Two patterns are
visible.  The rollout time per generation is largely independent of the number
of environments (the batched simulator absorbs the extra environments almost
for free), whereas the update time grows with both the minibatch size
and the volume of collected data, and dominates the budget at the larger
configurations.  Increasing the minibatch size markedly improves overall
throughput (e.g.\ at $256$ environments, from $6.5 \times 10^{3}$ steps
per second at minibatch $64$ to $4.9 \times 10^{4}$ at minibatch $4096$),
because larger minibatches amortize the fixed per-update overhead of the
CPU-resident optimizer.  The single-environment rows isolate that overhead: the
rollout collapses to one long serial trajectory (3.3\,s) that caps
throughput near $10^{3}$ steps per second regardless of minibatch size.

The remaining gap identifies the next bottleneck.  As
Table~\ref{tab:wrapper_overhead} shows, the \texttt{VSSVecEnv} wrapper crosses
a Python and Gymnasium boundary at every step, converting between JAX arrays
and NumPy arrays, at a near-constant cost of $0.6$--$0.9$\,ms per step.  The
full SB3 path adds CPU-bound PyTorch gradient updates, so the learner cannot
stay on the same accelerator as the simulator.  The TG-1 simulator removes the original physics and socket-communication
bottleneck; the learner-side boundary now dominates.  A JAX-native training path~\cite{hessel2021podracer,toledo2024stoix} is
planned for TG-2 to close this gap by keeping policy inference, rollout
collection, and optimization on device.

These results confirm that expressing VSSS physics as batched array operations
yields throughput well above what a conventional single-instance simulator can
achieve, and identify the learner-side boundary as the next bottleneck to
address.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Simulator Interface and Architecture}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The second preliminary result is the simulator as a software artifact: a
validated, reusable environment that the learner can call while preserving the
vectorization required for high throughput.

The simulator's training path is the JAX backend: its state is an immutable
\texttt{NamedTuple} PyTree using \texttt{float32} arithmetic, directly
compatible with \texttt{jax.vmap} for batched execution.  A CPU reference
implementation exists for unit testing and debugging but is not part of the
training path.  Run-specific training settings are tabulated in
Table~\ref{tab:smoke_config} (Section~\ref{sec:early_training}).

On top of the physics core, the environment exposes the reset–step interface
described in Section~\ref{sec:simulator} (Chapter~4), registered through the
Gymnasium vectorized-environment API and bridged to Stable-Baselines3 via a
thin adapter.

The per-step data flow is:
%
\begin{enumerate}
    \item initialize (or reset) a batch of game states;
    \item run policy inference for the controlled team in each environment;
    \item step the batched physics on device;
    \item compute observations, rewards, and termination flags;
    \item accumulate transitions for the \gls{ppo} update.
\end{enumerate}
%
The observation and action spaces are detailed in Sections~\ref{sec:obs_space} and~\ref{sec:action_space} (Chapter~4); both constructions are factored as swappable functions so the interface supports per-robot egocentric observations without changes to the simulator.

The robustness of this interface is supported by an automated test suite of 174
unit tests (with 4 GPU-specific tests skipped in the absence of a GPU) covering
physics correctness, Gymnasium API compliance, batched stepping and auto-reset,
and the SB3 adapter protocol.  The simulator is therefore a validated, reusable artifact: straightforward to
call from a learner while preserving the vectorization that the throughput
results depend on.
Figure~\ref{fig:pygame_window} shows the Pygame-based renderer used during
development to visualise running matches and trained policies.

\subsection{Early Training Validation}
\label{sec:early_training}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

A final preliminary result is an end-to-end training smoke test confirming that
the \gls{ppo} loop runs correctly on the new simulator.  Using the batched
environment through the SB3 adapter, a Stage-1 configuration (the controlled
team alone, against stationary opponents) was trained with the shaped reward
described in Chapter~4: the sparse $\pm 1$ goal signal augmented by a small
dense term proportional to the ball's forward progress, with the shaping
coefficient set so that its total episodic contribution cannot exceed a single
goal event.  Training runs are tracked with MLflow, logging per-generation mean,
minimum, and maximum reward together with episode length.
For these runs, with 256 environments and a 300-step episode cap, one
generation collects $256 \times 300 = 76{,}800$ environment transitions.

These early training experiments serve to confirm that rollout collection,
advantage estimation, optimization, and checkpointing operate correctly on the
new infrastructure, not to produce a competitive policy.

Both runs were executed on a MacBook with an Apple M4 Pro chip,
using the batched JAX backend.

validation rather than a performance result.

too sparse a signal to drive learning.  The \gls{plr} sampler is designed to
interpolate between these extremes: it steers the distribution of initial
states toward configurations where the current policy's value-prediction error
is largest, balancing sufficient difficulty with sufficient learning signal.

\section{Future Planned Results}
\label{sec:future_planned_results}
% ==================================================================

The infrastructure validated above is the foundation for the experiments
planned for TG-2.  This section states what those experiments are and what each
is intended to show.

\subsection{Closing the throughput gap with a JAX-native learner.}
The throughput measurements identified two ceilings: the Gymnasium boundary and
the CPU-bound PyTorch updates of SB3.  Both stem from the learner living outside
the accelerator.  The planned remedy is a fully JAX-resident training path
following the Anakin podracer architecture~\cite{hessel2021podracer}, in which
the policy, rollout collection, and gradient updates all execute on device
alongside the simulator, removing per-step host transfers entirely.
Stoix~\cite{toledo2024stoix} provides a compiled \gls{ppo} implementation that
supports this regime directly.  The target is to bring end-to-end \gls{ppo}
throughput closer to the raw batched-kernel ceiling --- on the order of
$5 \times 10^{6}$ steps per second on the RTX~3060, against the present peak
of about $6 \times 10^{4}$ of the SB3 path.  The expected result is a
before/after throughput comparison of the full training loop, not merely the
simulator in isolation.

\subsection{Prioritized-level-replay sample efficiency.}
With the sampler integrated, the central evaluation is whether prioritizing
high-learning-potential scenarios improves sample efficiency relative to uniform
initialization.  The planned result is a set of learning curves (reward and goal
rate versus environment steps) comparing the \gls{plr} sampler against a uniform
baseline under identical compute budgets, accompanied by qualitative inspection
of which scenarios the sampler prioritizes over the course of training.

\subsection{Two-stage curriculum and self-play.}
The opponents are currently stationary.  TG-2 will introduce the second
curriculum stage, replacing them with self-play opponents drawn from a history
of past policies, and will evaluate the effect of the opponent-sampling scheme
on the robustness of the learned team.  The planned result is a comparison of
policies trained with and without the curriculum, and with different
opponent-sampling strategies.

\subsection{Symmetry exploitation.}
TG-2 will quantify the benefit of the permutation- and reflection-symmetry
mechanisms described in Chapter~4, through an ablation comparing training with
and without episode-level permutation and reflection augmentation.  Exact
parameter sharing remains a separate experiment for the later decentralized
per-robot formulation.

\subsection{Evaluation against the classical strategy.}
Finally, the trained team will be evaluated against the ITAndroids classical
strategy stack, reporting win rate, goal difference, and qualitative behavioral
analysis — the measure of whether a learned 3v3 team policy is competitive with
the hand-engineered baseline.  Sim-to-real considerations (domain randomization,
noise injection) frame this evaluation as a step toward eventual deployment, the
longer-term goal beyond TG-2.
